# 0017: A check number both people confirm before the file goes

- Status: Accepted
- Date: 2026-11-25
- Linear: SIG-109

## Context

- 0012 lets Fidant email a link, key included (its option 3). With the key, our server can open that link's sealed setup messages, swap the DTLS fingerprints and sit in the middle of the peer-to-peer connection, or open the link itself and act as the recipient. 0012 accepted this ("Fidant can act as the recipient of an emailed link") and named check words (its option 6) as the fix, in SIG-109.
- 0012 set two conditions for them. They come only from the DTLS certificate fingerprints that each browser's own connection used, never from the link secret. And the server must not be able to pick its certificate after it learns what they depend on: either a commitment first, or about 64 bits.
- 0014 made emailing a link opt-in. Its notice on the send page, and the site copy that 0014 kept in body text, say that until check words exist our server could sit in the middle of an emailed link's transfer.
- Our server also serves the page. A number on the page can show that nobody sat in the middle of the connection; it can't show that the page itself is honest.
- `apps/e2e` runs Chromium only. Before building, a spike in Chromium 159, Firefox 159 and Playwright's WebKit (Safari 27.2) made a certificate with `RTCPeerConnection.generateCertificate` and read its fingerprint with `RTCCertificate.getFingerprints()`. In all three it was one sha-256 value, equal (upper-cased) to the `a=fingerprint:sha-256` line of an offer made with that certificate.

## Options considered

| Option                                                                                  | For                                                                                                                                                                    | Against                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. The recipient commits to its certificate first, and both screens show a short number | Someone in the middle must fix both certificates before it learns what the number depends on, so each connection is one blind guess. Six digits give one in a million. | One more sealed signal. Each side makes its certificate up front, and the offer waits for the commit.                                                                                                 |
| 2. No commitment, a long number                                                         | No protocol change.                                                                                                                                                    | About 64 bits to read aloud (20 digits, or 6 words from a 2,048-word list), and safety that rests on no certificate search finishing while the handshake waits. That weakens as hardware gets faster. |
| 3. A key exchange inside the data channel                                               | Independent of WebRTC's fingerprints.                                                                                                                                  | 0012 requires the words to come from the DTLS fingerprints, and a second key exchange adds crypto for nothing.                                                                                        |

Digits rather than words, because they read the same on Swedish and English pages.

Once option 1 was built, a hostile server could still make the recipient's page connect again and again: it learns the recipient's number from the recipient's answer, before that channel opens, and could hold the sender's attempt open meanwhile. The page reconnected by itself 5 times per load after a dropped socket, and answering its `join` with `gate`, then `gate` with an address and a `token` it never asked for, reset that count: one page load made 500 fresh connections in about 2 seconds. Each connection is another guess. We weighed:

- **The recipient's page reconnects only on the recipient's click once it answered, ignores a token it didn't ask for, and an unconfirmed check ends after 5 minutes (chosen).** Every guess after the first in an attempt costs a visible failed connection and a click.
- **The same, plus a cap of 5 failed checks per link in the recipient's tab.** About 25 guesses per tab, but a second limit to explain, and a new tab escapes it.
- **Only the reconnect rule, no deadline.** One sender attempt could wait in "checking" for as long as the recipient keeps clicking.
- **No change, a weaker claim.** Hundreds of silent guesses per page load.

## Decision

Option 1, with the reconnect rule and the deadline.

- **Who turns it on.** The sender chooses per recipient when adding them: "Check a number with this recipient before the file goes" ("before the secret goes" on `/secret`). It is ticked by default for a recipient Fidant emails and unticked for a link the sender passes on themselves.
- **Signaling, every connection, check on or off.** Each side makes a fresh ECDSA P-256 certificate for each connection. The recipient first sends `{kind: "commit", hash}`, sealed like every signal, with `hash = base64url(SHA-256("fidant-commit-v1\n" ‖ fingerprint))`. The sender makes its offer only after the commit arrives, and the offer says whether this recipient's check is on (`check`). The sender refuses a second commit and an answer whose fingerprint does not hash to the commit (`bad-signal`). Every fingerprint line of an SDP, in any spelling, must be the exact `a=fingerprint:sha-256 <value>` form, and all of them must name one certificate, or the page refuses the SDP before the browser sees it. After the browser parses the other side's description, the page reads the fingerprint back from the browser's own copy and refuses any difference, because browsers disagree about which odd spellings they still read. The recipient also refuses to answer unless its answer names the certificate it committed to. A fingerprint is the upper-case colon hex the SDP carries. There is no compatibility mode for pages loaded before this change: accepting the old flow would let the server strip the commit.
- **The number.** Each page reads the two fingerprints from its own offer and answer, the ones its browser checks the DTLS handshake against. The number is the first 8 bytes of `SHA-256("fidant-check-v1\n" ‖ offer fingerprint ‖ "\n" ‖ answer fingerprint)` as an unsigned big-endian integer, modulo 1,000,000, with leading zeros, shown as two groups: `482 913`. It is shown on screen and never sent, logged or stored.
- **Who compares.** The recipient reads their number aloud and the sender compares it with their own screen, so the person whose file it is does the comparing. The sender's buttons are "It matches" and "It differs"; the recipient's are "They said it matches" and "They said it differs".
- **Confirming.** Two new frames, CHECK from the sender (kind 4) and from the recipient (kind 20), encrypted like every other frame, with a 1-byte body: 1 matches, 0 differs. With the check on, both pages hold META and everything after it until the sender's own click and the recipient's CHECK 1 have both happened, and the recipient's page shows no offer until its own click and the sender's CHECK 1. Either side's CHECK 0 ends the attempt on both pages, and the link still works, because a misheard digit is the likely cause.
- **The attempt cap.** The sender's page counts, per link with the check on, every attempt that ended before both confirmed: a drop, a timeout, the recipient leaving, a server drop, a refused relay, a connection too small for a shared secret, a mismatch, or a check not confirmed within 5 minutes. At 5 it stops hosting the link, and the card says so and suggests adding the recipient again. The count stays in the page.
- **The recipient's reconnects.** Once the recipient's page has sent its answer, it never makes a new connection by itself: a socket that drops before the channel opens ends the attempt, and only "Try again" or a reload makes a new one. Before the answer a new connection reveals nothing, so the existing automatic rejoins stay. A `token` counts only as the answer to a code the page sent.

## Consequences

- **0012 and 0014 are amended, not superseded, and stay Accepted.** 0012's consequence "Fidant can act as the recipient of an emailed link" and the copy 0014 governs (the send page's email notice and the body text on /how, /security, /privacy and the technical pages) now hold only for an emailed link sent with the check number turned off. With the check on, a server in the middle or acting as the recipient shows the sender a number the real recipient doesn't have. The rest of both records stands.
- **What the check proves.** That nobody sat in the middle of this one connection, our server included, when the two numbers matched. It does not prove that the page code is honest: a page that skips the check can't be caught by it. Copy never claims more than that.
- **The sender's click protects the file.** Someone in the middle is one end of the sender's encrypted channel, so it can send the sender a CHECK 1 of its own. The sender's card never shows the recipient's answer as a sign the check passed, and no copy says the recipient's confirmation protects the file. The recipient's click protects the recipient only when the offer arrives with the check on: it holds back a file that someone in the middle sends. Someone in the middle writes the offer and can set the check off, and then the recipient's page shows no number and asks for no click.
- **How many guesses.** Each new connection, on either side, gives someone in the middle one blind guess: one in a million. The sender's side allows at most 5 unconfirmed attempts per link, each at most 5 minutes. On the recipient's side, every connection after the first in an attempt needs the recipient to see a failed connection and click "Try again". Ten connections in all give at most 1 in 100,000. There is no fixed bound per link, because the recipient can keep clicking.
- **A flipped flag fails closed.** For an emailed link the server can change the offer's `check`. Then one page shows a number and the other doesn't, the page that expects CHECK gets META or the other way round, and nothing about the file is shown.
- **It costs a conversation.** The two people must talk while both pages are open. A sender who turns it off, or clicks "It matches" without comparing, gets no protection from it. It does not stop a mail scanner from holding an emailed link (0012).
- **Pages from before this change can't connect to pages after it.** Both reload.
- **Tests.** Unit tests cover the derivation against fixed vectors, the commit refusals, the CHECK frames, holding META for both confirmations and the attempt counter. End-to-end tests in Chromium cover the honest paths, the reconnect rule, an unasked token and a man-in-the-middle harness holding an emailed link's key, whose two pages show different numbers and receive no META. Firefox and WebKit in Playwright are a separate issue.
- **Docs.** `docs/how-fidant-works.md` describes the commit, the number, the CHECK frames, the cap, the reconnect rule and what the check does not prove, and the web text says the same in English and Swedish.
